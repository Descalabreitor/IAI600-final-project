\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Data and Preparation}
\label{sec:data}
The data is the Kaggle ``Bitcoin Historical Data'' set~\cite{kaggle} of
1-minute Bitstamp BTC/USD candles, pinned to one version and aggregated
to hourly candles without dropping missing rows, so the hourly grid stays
continuous. The minute file hides its gaps as flat, zero-volume candles
at the previous close: 95\% of the minutes of 2012 and 25--40\% of
2013--2016, but 1--4\% per year since 2018. Outage hours are flagged, not
removed, and we use the 59{,}331 hours from 1 January 2020 to October
2026, so that training is close to the current market. Extra inputs were
tested: Binance BTC and ETH candles with order flow~\cite{binance}, about
40 macro series from FRED~\cite{fred} and Yahoo Finance, and the times of
US CPI, payroll and FOMC releases.

\subsection{Exploratory Data Analysis}
\label{sec:eda}
Every analysis that could steer a model is fitted on the training block
and checked on validation. Fig.~\ref{fig:acf} shows the central fact:
returns are almost uncorrelated (about $-0.03$ at lags 1, 2 and 24; an
AR(24) model does worse than the mean on validation), while their
absolute values are strongly autocorrelated (0.30 at lag 1, still 0.10 a
week later). The direction is almost noise; the size is not. The size
also follows the clock, and this rhythm drifts (Fig.~\ref{fig:hour}): the
14~UTC peak of the US session grows from 2022 and jumps after the spot
ETFs, weekends become calmer, and the average hourly move falls from
0.50\% in training to about 0.30\% later. US releases move Bitcoin 2--3
times more than the same hour on other days, whereas ETH and the macro
variables move together with Bitcoin but never lead it.
Table~\ref{tab:eda} lists how each finding shaped the modelling.

\subsection{Targets and Features}
\label{sec:targets}
A forecast is made at every hour (the \emph{origin}) with 30 days of
history and 24 target hours. Besides the 24 returns we forecast (a) the
direction of the next hour, (b) the size $|\ret|$ of each of the next 24
hours, (c) an 80\% range for each of them and (d) the next day's return,
direction and volatility. Features are lagged returns, realised
volatilities over 24--168 hours, high--low ranges, volume ratios and the
calendar. In the \emph{long format} each origin gives 24 rows, one per
predicted hour, with columns for that hour (its average size, its size a
day and a week earlier): one model learns the common structure
(``volatile weeks make every hour volatile'') from 24 times more rows.

\subsection{Models}
\label{sec:models}
\emph{Classical:} Ridge~\cite{hoerl1970}, Lasso~\cite{tibshirani1996},
ElasticNet~\cite{zou2005}, $k$-nearest neighbours, SVR/SVC, Random
Forest~\cite{breiman2001}, Extra Trees~\cite{geurts2006} and Gradient
Boosting (GB)~\cite{friedman2001}, alone and averaged. Ranges come from GB
quantile regression~\cite{koenker1978} (10\% and 90\%), recalibrated by
\emph{rolling conformal calibration}~\cite{vovk2005,gibbs2021}: the
half-width is rescaled so that the last 30 days of known outcomes would
have had 80\% coverage; the \emph{Mondrian} variant does this separately
for release hours. Volatility models are also refitted every month on
all data known by then. \emph{Neural:} MLPs and LSTMs~\cite{hochreiter1997}
reading a window-normalised 168-hour window; the best LSTM also sees the
clock and release flags of each predicted hour, weights the week by
traded volume (attention) and is fine-tuned monthly. \emph{Foundation
model:} Timestral~\cite{timestral}, pretrained by Mistral AI on mostly
synthetic series, returns 21 quantiles of each of the next 24 hours. It
is used zero-shot: we only choose on validation what it reads (series,
context length, covariates) and its size. Table~\ref{tab:hyper} lists the
chosen settings.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The analysis of the data decided most of the project: it showed that
hourly returns are close to a random walk, so the assigned target cannot
be beaten by any model, and that the size of the moves is persistent,
clock-driven and drifting, which pointed to scale-free volatility
features, one row per predicted hour, monthly refits and rolling
calibration. We therefore recommend answering the task with the expected
size and an 80\% range for each hour, for which the average of
Timestral and monthly-refitted Gradient Boosting explains 2.9 times the
variance of a volatility rule. These forecasts control trading losses,
not returns.

We learned that the evaluation design matters more than the model:
chronological blocks with a purge, choices made without the test block,
simple baselines and bootstrap intervals separate real signal from
noise. Recency beat data volume and extra sources, and a pretrained
model, used as it comes, matched careful feature engineering where there
was signal but could not create signal where there was none.

The study has one test period of 2.25 years, many tests on the returns
without a multiple-testing correction, release hours that remain hard to
forecast, and a trading simulation without slippage or funding costs.
Timestral was released after our test block without listing its
training series, so its scores may be optimistic. Future work includes
a rolling-origin evaluation over 2023--2026, a cost-aware test of the
next-hour signal, a forward test of Timestral, and options, where the
size of a move pays without its direction.

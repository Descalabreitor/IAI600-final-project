\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The analysis of the data decided most of the project: it showed that
hourly returns are close to a random walk, so that none of the models we
tried on the assigned target, from linear models to a pretrained
foundation model, beats the zero forecast, and that the size of the
moves is persistent, clock-driven and drifting, which pointed to
scale-free volatility features, one row per predicted hour, monthly
refits and rolling calibration. We therefore recommend answering the
task with the expected size and an 80\% range for each hour, for which
the average of Timestral and monthly-refitted Gradient Boosting explains
2.3 times the variance of the best simple rule. These forecasts control
trading losses, not returns.

We learned that the evaluation design matters more than the model:
chronological blocks with a purge, choices made without the test block,
simple baselines and bootstrap intervals separate real signal from
noise. Recency beat data volume and extra sources, and a pretrained
model, used as it comes, matched careful feature engineering where there
was signal but could not create signal where there was none.

The study has one test period of 2.25 years, many tests on the returns
without a multiple-testing correction, release hours that remain hard to
forecast, and a trading simulation without slippage or funding costs.
Timestral's second training stage used real series that are not listed;
if they include Bitcoin after mid-2024, its test scores may be
optimistic. Future work includes a rolling-origin evaluation over
2023--2026, a cost-aware test of the next-hour signal, a forward test of
Timestral, other deep backbones~\cite{wang2024deep}, and options, where
the size of a move pays without its direction.

\subsection{Returns and Direction}
\label{sec:res_returns}
No model beats ``no change'' for the hourly returns: every tuned model
ends within rounding of the zero forecast's MAE, and more model freedom
always meant worse validation scores; the chosen Ridge shrinks almost to
zero. Selecting by squared error instead of MAE leaves a tiny ranking
signal (IC 0.02, interval excluding zero) that is far too weak to beat
zero, as market efficiency predicts~\cite{fama1970}. The one exception
is the next hour, which tends to reverse the last one: the simple rule
reaches AUC 0.528, GB with Binance order flow 0.538. The effect is small,
before trading costs, and gone from the second hour on. The weak lag-1
autocorrelation of Fig.~\ref{fig:acf} does not contradict it: the
autocorrelation is dominated by a few huge hours, whereas the AUC counts
correct signs.

\subsection{Size of the Hourly Moves}
\label{sec:res_size}
The size is where the models have clear skill (Fig.~\ref{fig:size}). The
long format was the largest modelling gain (validation $\Rtwo$ 0.052 to
0.097). On test, GB fitted once explains 13.1\% of the variance of each
hourly $|\ret|$, more than twice the volatility rule. Refitting every
month raises it to 14.8\% ($+0.017$ $[+0.010, +0.024]$): in a drifting
market, keeping the model current is worth more than any extra input.
None of the candidate features from the analysis, nor Binance and ETH,
improved the trees, because recent volatility and the clock already carry
their information. Release hours stay under-predicted: with a few
hundred training rows they are fewer than one leaf of the tuned GB.

Networks needed the same knowledge to compete: an LSTM gains $+0.031$
when told the clock and release flags of the hour it predicts (what the
per-hour columns do for the trees), and with volume attention and
monthly fine-tuning reaches 0.156, narrowly above GB. Timestral, never
trained on our data, matches it (0.157), provided it reads $|\ret|$
rather than $\ret$, a long (180-day) context and the release calendar;
the 209M model added almost nothing over the 28M one. The plain average
of Timestral and GB is the best result (0.164, $+0.016$ $[+0.013,
+0.020]$ over GB). Feeding Timestral's forecast into GB as a feature was
worse, and model families converge within 0.01 $\Rtwo$: averaging two
different families is what helps.

\subsection{Prediction Ranges and Next-Day Volatility}
\label{sec:res_ranges}
The GB quantile ranges are 28\% narrower than a fixed range and adapt to
calm and wild markets, but cover only 78\% of the test hours, because the
test years differ from the training years. A correction measured once
on validation does not fix this; rolling conformal calibration, which
keeps re-measuring the miss rate, restores 80\%. Release hours, covered
only 56\% of the time, reach 82\% when calibrated apart (Mondrian).
Timestral's own quantiles are calibrated without any correction (80.3\%)
and, thanks to the release calendar, cover 70\% of release hours. At the
daily scale, volatility is predictable as well: a tree ensemble explains
51\% of the next day's log volatility against 9\% for last week's
average, and a monthly refit again helps (0.517).

\subsection{Trading Simulation}
\label{sec:res_trading}
As risk meters, the model forecasts are far better than last week's
volatility, but they do not earn more money: all four flows end close
together, the naive one slightly ahead, and no Sharpe difference is
reliable. A risk-sized long position can only grow or shrink, never take
the right side; in the bull market of validation, cutting it before
volatile days even cost money. The forecasts do make trading safer,
because they know \emph{when} risk is high: at the same average leverage
($\approx13\times$) the leverage lands on calm days, and the averaged
forecast is liquidated 3 times on test instead of 12. The chance of a
drawdown above 20\% within a year falls from 27\% to 21\%, and to
10--12\% with a forecast-shaped intraday stop and a weekly loss limit,
against 54\% for the same trend rule without risk control. Timestral
does not predict the direction either: trading on its expected return
lost money.
